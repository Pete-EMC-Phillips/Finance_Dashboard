How this is worked out

All of your income streams are added together, because HMRC's personal allowance taper looks at your total adjusted net income — salary from every job or pension, plus taxable benefits, minus gross pension contributions and Gift Aid. The code below reflects that combined total, but in practice only one of your jobs (usually your main one) is actually given a personal-allowance code; HMRC typically taxes the others at a flat rate instead, such as BR, D0, or D1, with no allowance of their own.

If your salary or pension contribution changed partway through the year, the start-of-year figures apply from April up to the month you pick, and the current figures apply from that month to March. The tax code is worked out on what you're actually paid across the whole year, and take-home is worked out month by month the way payroll does it. Income Tax is cumulative: each month, payroll compares your pay so far this year with the tax-free pay and bands for the year so far, so a pay rise is taxed correctly from the month it starts. National Insurance and student loan are worked out on each month's pay on its own.

Once adjusted net income passes £100,000, the standard £12,570 Personal Allowance is cut by £1 for every £2 over that line, reaching zero at £125,140.

Benefits reported on a P11D are taxable but usually aren't taxed through your payslip as you earn them, so HMRC collects that tax by deducting the P11D value from your tax-free amount instead. A beneficial loan works the same way: if your employer charges you less than HMRC's official rate of interest, the shortfall — loan balance × (official rate − rate you pay) — is itself a P11D benefit. Loans that never exceed £10,000 in the year are exempt entirely; above that, the whole balance counts, not just the excess. The official rate is set by HMRC and has moved before (it's been 3.75% since April 2025, though it can now change quarterly), so the field above is editable.

If you owe tax from an earlier year — the sort of thing that shows up on a P800 or a Simple Assessment — or HMRC is adjusting your code for other reasons, these show up on your coding notice or in the "Check your Income Tax" section of the HMRC app as an underpayment, an adjustment to rate band, or an estimated amount you owe this year. HMRC has already worked out how much allowance each of these is worth by the time it shows you the figure, so the tax code adjustments card on this page takes the numbers exactly as shown there and subtracts them directly from your tax-free amount, the same way a P11D benefit is — no further conversion needed. Professional subscriptions work the other way round: approved professional body fees are a tax-deductible expense, so they add to your allowance instead of reducing it. In practice HMRC also caps how much can be "coded out" this way — broadly no more than 50% of your pay in any pay period, and Simple Assessment debts over £3,000 usually can't be coded out at all — limits this tool doesn't apply.

Whatever tax-free amount is left, divided by 10 and rounded down, becomes the number in your code, with L for the standard allowance. If benefits or charges use up the entire allowance, the code switches to 0T (no free pay) or a K code, which adds notional income to what's taxed rather than giving any tax-free amount at all.

This is an estimate assuming no marriage allowance, blind person's allowance, or other adjustments not covered above — your actual coding notice from HMRC is the final word.
